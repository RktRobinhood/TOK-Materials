# Education review, round 1 — frozen baseline f1bfcf6

Reviewed 3 October 2026 for #36, before fixes. This is an instructional-design judgement against DESIGN.md, CH2–CH4 and the supplied `research/tok-mathematics-teaching.md`, not a claim that pupils have achieved these outcomes. The game supplements teacher instruction for DP1 learners, many reading in a second language. Its strongest feature is making students manipulate evidence and possibilities rather than recall terminology. Its weakest feature is the gap between what the interface lets a player do and what the narrator says they have learned.

The highest-priority repairs are conceptual correctness and readable feedback: Tower's English does not always mean its formal rules; the Oracle sometimes recommends treating a few successful tests as proof; one Tribunal case treats an alternative explanation as disproof; and successful encounters disappear before students can read their explanation. The overall four-lesson sequence is coherent and worth retaining.

## Detailed findings and source anchors

**F1 — Tower's premises do not consistently mean its formal atoms.** Browser, Tower d2, seed `education`: base “I am a postal runner”; facts “Postal runners report to the Postmistress every morning” and “The Postmistress is terrified of geese, and her friends never eat goose pie.” Choosing “I have eaten the famous goose pie” is labelled contradiction. The explanation infers “I have never met the Postmistress.” In `js/puzzles/tower.js:65`, the atom's positive label is “I know” and negative “I have never met”; these are not logical negations. At line 112, “her friends” maps to that same know/met atom. Reporting to someone, knowing them and being their friend are different claims. The player is penalised for a hidden premise the game never states. Use exact complementary labels and exact wording for each implication; inspect other atoms for the same drift. Do not merely change the explanation after the verdict.

**F2 — Valid Oracle hints teach the error the unit is meant to prevent.** `js/puzzles/oracle.js`, `hintsFor` valid branch: “Test every step with two or three different numbers” followed by “If no number breaks any step, stamp ‘every step holds’.” Its wrong-step feedback also says a step “actually holds” because a few numbers agree. Successful sampling cannot establish a universal algebraic step. Browser completed the *flawed* prime task correctly at n=40; that strength does not validate the unseen valid branch. Change instructions to seek a general rule/reason; tests can locate counterexamples. A concise algebraic justification can be supplied if checking it would otherwise test maths fluency rather than TOK thinking.

**F3 — Tribunal sometimes confuses undermining with disproving.** `data/cases.js`, case `twice-cat`, first contradiction and Why: a photograph showing two identical cats is treated as proof that the witness did not see the same cat twice and that their identity assumption is false. It proves the description alone cannot establish identity; it does not prove two different cats walked past. The case's final lesson correctly says a broken argument leaves the conclusion unsupported, making the earlier language especially confusing. State the limited evidential result throughout. Also scrutinise Library repair wording: “no hole” needs a stated domain of connected ordinary polyhedral surfaces, rather than a universal slogan about every solid.

**F4 — Success can remove the explanation before it teaches.** `js/screens/encounter.js`, `onSubmit`, advances in 1100 ms. `nextStage` asks Why only for the last stage of a boss/mini-boss; `victory` shows only that last definition's `tok`. In the Core, students may finish prediction and proof with neither idea available in the final explicit recap. Bench Oracle retains its explanation, so that browser experience must not be mistaken for main-game success flow. Add learner-controlled Continue and compact per-stage explanation/recap. This supports second-language readers and is more valuable than additional explanatory text that disappears.

**F5 — Reflection and diagram completion are not reliable evidence of learning.** Browser Venn d1 seed `education`: a Valid verdict on an entirely untouched board produces `solved:true`, `partial:0.75`, `diagram.perfect:false`, four missing shaded regions. This may be a legitimate choice if diagramming is optional, but the instructions and Syllo's script ask students to construct it; victory interrupts correction. `js/puzzles/three-act.js:477` regards any valid reflection index as sufficient; `checkAnswer` never checks its relation to the model/reveal. Reflection should permit honest uncertainty, but then offer specific feedback rather than certifying an arbitrary label as a meaningful explanation.

**F6 — Progress language attributes unobserved work.** Village `check` verifies accusations, not whether alternatives were eliminated using the table. `ch2.lane.win` says “You checked every world”; School/Hall make similar assertions; encounter rewards award `truth-tabler` for any Village success. The Village table itself is thoughtfully scaffolded. Preserve alternate valid deduction approaches; make outcome claims truthful and distinguish solving from demonstrating proof by exhaustion.

**F7 — Random task and fixed script sometimes diverge.** Stone's pattern/counterexample story can accompany a dot or solvable graph; Gate includes Venn among three guards-themed stages; Hall's Switchboard stage sits inside villager accusations; Schoolhouse can switch families. `data/map.js` selects/defines these tasks but fixed `.win` lines assume one mode. Tell students what the current task is demonstrating and debrief that mode. This is particularly important when joining a chapter out of sequence.

**F8 — Concept teaching is sometimes in costly hints.** Graph parity, Venn existential marks, circuit operators and algebraic restrictions need a free explanation before students must use them. Charging health for strategic assistance is an agreed game design; charging for the definition of a required concept is an instructional barrier. Separate the two without eliminating consequences. Wrong-answer feedback in Witness should use its existing per-claim reasons before adding more punishments or timers.

**F9 — Several memorable lines need scope qualifiers.** `prologue.gallery.win` says truth means the world agrees and mathematics needs validity plus truth; `ch2.clockmaker.win` gives every argument a truth table; Switchboard equates fewer ON inputs with universally stronger argument; prediction/core categorical statements treat this toy's counts as all AI or as inability to support knowledge. These analogies are useful within stipulated systems, but do not settle the philosophical questions. Use “in this game”, “for this circuit”, “given these premises” and “prediction is not a guarantee”. Keep the agreed villain/frequency-model design; wider changes to the lesson's position require the teacher's judgement. Do not teach the liar paradox as Gödel's incompleteness theorem.

**F10 — Transfer is asserted more often than elicited.** Finale contains a memorable conclusion but no student example/justification. Card axioms model changing systems but no comparison makes it explicit. Sorting sets three fairness goals without asking who selected them. A small reflective prompt using an encounter already played could connect proof, evidence, models and responsibility without extending the adventure into a large written assignment.

## Across the four lessons

The planned progression is sound: lesson 1 supplies inference, counterexamples and hidden assumptions; lesson 2 turns those into exhaustive formal checks and dependent claims; lesson 3 applies them to arguments, statistical evidence and theorem repair; lesson 4 combines representation, prediction, ethics, proof checking and modelling. Village cell inspection, Tribunal evidence matching, Sorting's audit and Chart Fixer's repair-plus-reading are especially well aligned: the intended reasoning is visible in the student's actions.

Repeated Venn/Rule Hunter in Ch1 and Ch3 and chart repair in Ch3/Ch4 can provide useful retrieval and greater complexity. Without variant-specific recaps, repetition can feel like more of the same rather than transfer. Boss chains are natural synthesis points but should explicitly compare their methods. An exhaustive finite table is proof relative to formal rules; a statistical model is evaluated against data; a proof's universal steps need reasons. These should not blur into a generic “checking” slogan.

Open chapter travel is deliberate. Therefore no core explanation should rely on a pupil having visited an optional stall, seen a particular random variant or read an earlier lecture. Short first-use recaps are sufficient; the teacher's lesson can deepen them. This review does not propose restricting the open map or turning the game into a mathematical-fluency test.

Against the supplied TOK research, **methods/tools** are strongest. **Ethics** has a substantive Sorting task, but could better connect responsibility and chosen error costs to the player's own reasoning. **Perspectives** and **scope** are thinner: historical legendaries mostly decorate, the proof-checker prompts open community questions without sustained comparison, and logic occupies much more space than other mathematical practice. Not every proposed research example belongs in a four-lesson follow-up. Prioritise one explicit maths bridge in each chapter, one social/technological proof question and an exit comparison over adding many new activities.

The narrative makes confidence and charisma suspicious, usefully, but epistemic humility should apply to the heroes too. Testimony can be evidence; a famous person's claim can be true; a valid derivation does not establish its premises; a failed argument does not prove its conclusion false.

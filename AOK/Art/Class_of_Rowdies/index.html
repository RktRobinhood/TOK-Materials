    What the piece is doing
    Apart from a 120-millisecond “WAKE UP.” inside the blackout shock — felt more than read — nothing on the page states a lesson. The film shows a “rowdy” class whose energy gets somewhere once a room is built to listen. The story after it runs on one image: current. The same electricity can short out and shock, light a neon sign (made to be looked at), or feed a lamp (light you can look by). The music does the same: the 1917 jazz record that is muffled, warped and overdriven in the speakeasy comes back clean at the end. The speakeasy stands in for a moratorium — a stretch of life where nobody checks the time and consequences seem suspended. Each fork offers two equally energetic uses of the same moment. Neon choices feel good — a pink bloom, a cheer — and quietly add a bar to a cage around the screen; at the tally the speakeasy's own signs (NO CLOCKS, OPEN ALL NIGHT…) turn out to be bars too. Letting the fuse burn out is also a choice — the room makes it for you. Shocks land on moments of recognition that every reader reaches, never on a choice.

    Discussion starters
    Where did your energy go at each fork, and did it feel like a choice at the time? Which bars were yours and which were already there? What would “wiring a lamp” look like in our actual room on a normal Tuesday? Can a piece of art make an argument without saying it? What does it lose or gain by refusing to?

    A footnote for the heckle fork
    “Impressionism” began as a sneer: the critic Louis Leroy took the title of Monet’s Impression, Sunrise — the painting at the end of this story — and used it to mock the whole exhibition in 1874. The heckled became the canon.

    Intensity
    Sudden sounds, screen-shake and single white flashes. Flashes are rate-limited (never more than two luminance changes per shock, at least 1.1 s apart). “shocks: gentle” removes shake and lightning and softens flashes; reduced-motion settings switch it on automatically.

    What the piece is doing
    The film shows a class everyone has written off, and a teacher who builds a room where each of them turns out to have something to bring. The page runs parallel: a loud, turbulent class seen by one student in it, and sometimes from the front of the room. It takes the students’ side — the joke really is funny, the hand really is scary to raise — and it is reciprocal: the teacher answers what the class does, so the same room can become a battle or something good. Choices that feed the noise lay a brick in a wall along the bottom of the screen; the room adds its own bricks, and in a battle the teacher lays a couple too (“SIT THERE”, “WORKSHEET”). The wall only comes down when the reader answers “What would you bring?”. A Kantian turn shows every choice multiplied across all twenty-eight seats. The shocks land on moments of recognition — never on a choice. The piece ends on a recruitment poster and an enlistment card: what could you give, what do you want to get, whose skill would you learn, what from your bucket list belongs in the course. The last question — one thing you’ll do every week — is deliberately never printed.

    Discussion starters
    When does a teacher stop pushing for your future, and when do you start? If everyone in the room made your choice, what would the room be? Which bricks in the wall were already there before you arrived? Can a piece of art make an argument without saying it? What would you actually bring on a Tuesday?

    A footnote for the art
    Jan Steen painted riotous classrooms in the 1670s; Bruegel’s Children’s Games (1560) shows over eighty games at once — chaos, or a catalogue of skills, depending on how long you look. Hammershøi painted his Copenhagen flat at Strandgade 30 as a sequence of open doors with nobody walking through them.

    Intensity
    Sudden sounds, screen-shake and single white flashes. Flashes are rate-limited (never more than two luminance changes per shock, at least 1.1 s apart). “shocks: gentle” removes shake and lightning and softens flashes; reduced-motion settings switch it on automatically.

A 70-minute inquiry into education, evidence and personal learning
Phase 1 · Frame the question

If attendance were not the organising principle of education, how would you learn?

Assume your ambitions stay high: strong results, university options, genuine competence and the ability to perform when it matters. Remove only one assumption—that effective education must be organised around everyone spending the same number of hours in the same place.

What would you keep from your current education, what would you change, and what would you need evidence for before changing it?

Start with your own education

Think of one area where you are already motivated to improve. It might be mathematics, an essay, a language, remembering content, understanding difficult concepts or preparing for an examination.

That is your test case. Today is not about designing an imaginary school. It is about investigating whether any of these ideas could make you better at something you actually care about.

Useful questions

Claim: What exactly are they saying improves?
Evidence: What would distinguish real learning from short-term performance?
Cause: What else could explain the results?
Transfer: Would the mechanism work for your subject, your habits, and your constraints?

Sport as a comparison

If useful, compare the learning process to training: targeted practice, immediate feedback, video review, coaching, progressive difficulty, recovery, repetition. Then notice where the analogy stops working.

Phase 3 · Develop a model

Build a learning process you would actually test

Use what survived your investigation. You do not need to adopt Alpha's model, use AI, or reject school. Design a loop for your own test case that could plausibly improve the quality of your learning.

Before you sketch

You can think on paper, in the box below, or directly on the sketch. These are starting points, not questions to answer one by one.

  • What do you currently do that is mostly time spent rather than evidence of learning?
  • Where could you get faster or better feedback?
  • What would make practice progressively harder rather than merely repetitive?
  • How would you know a week later that the learning lasted and could transfer?

Sketch your learning loop

Use arrows, cycles, labels, examples, sport parallels—whatever makes the process clear.

Where does a meaningful attempt happen?
Where does feedback enter?
What changes before the next attempt?
How do you test retention or transfer?

# Three quick capture games for issue #158

Research date: 2026-10-18. Research and design proposal for [#158](https://github.com/RktRobinhood/TOK-Materials/issues/158), including its teacher comments. **No prototype or win-rate measurement was performed for this note.** [Verified] means checked against the linked primary source; [proposal] means our untested design/tuning. The teacher's existing five unsuccessful attempts are evidence about the old game, not an estimate of the proposed game's success rate.

## Current human direction — updated 2026-10-18

**The user's latest direct instruction supersedes the earlier issue comments requiring one mechanic. Build THREE separate quick games; choose the game randomly for each catch; scale each game's difficulty by creature rarity.** The desired references are simple, compact Atari/Windows classics. The older single-arena proposal below is retained as design history for game 1, not a constraint against games 2 and 3. No additional teacher approval is needed to research or prototype the newly requested trio; the fun/playtest verdict still matters before treating it as finished.

**[Proposal] Recommended trio: net cuts, ricochet catch, and a tiny deduction trail.** These test spatial planning, aim/tracking, and deduction respectively. Keep one small catch window, one-line prompts, clear progress and immediate replay feedback. Aim initially for **15–40 seconds** in ordinary play; this is proposed tuning, not a duration established by the historical games. Calm must allow extra thinking time. Random game/layout selection provides variety; the outcome must follow the player's actions, never a second catch-success coin flip.

### Game 2: ricochet catch — Breakout reference

**[Verified]** [Atari's own Breakout instructions](https://atari.com/pages/breakout) describe moving a bottom paddle to bounce a ball into destructible bricks and losing a ball when the paddle misses. Importantly, their **Catch** variation lets the player hold the ball on the paddle to plan and aim the next shot. This is evidence for the documented home version; its five-ball/two-wall structure is not a suitable duration guarantee for our short catch. Atari's [historical account](https://atari.com/blogs/atari/new-insight-into-breakout-s-origins) describes its relationship to Pong and Atari's legacy. That establishes provenance, not classroom appeal or independent review scores.

**[Proposal]** One-line prompt: **“Bounce the spark into every seal—keep it on your net.”** One ball, one paddle, four to six large, single-hit seals, and two misses allowed. Clearing the seals closes the creature's cage and wins. Keep a visible miss counter and seal count; no inventory, new power-ups or endless brick walls. A brief serve/aim preview teaches the first bounce. Try a finite rally budget so an unlucky shallow loop cannot drag on indefinitely; show that budget from the start. Tune flight length and seal placement for a handful of useful returns, then measure actual time.

- Mouse/touch sets a target paddle position in a bottom control strip; left/right keys move toward the same positions. Apply the **same maximum paddle speed** to every input so a mouse cannot teleport while keyboard has to travel. A large Serve button and Enter/Space launch the held ball. No dragging, right-click or hover-only instruction is required; the finger should not cover the ball.
- Rarity adjusts a few numbers: paddle width, ball speed, seal count/spacing, rally budget and misses. Keep collision and aiming rules the same; avoid introducing extra balls or invisible targets for a rarity. A charm can widen the net; a lure can grant a clearly displayed extra miss. Verify improvement on paired layouts rather than assume it.
- Calm uses a slower time scale and an optional hold-at-paddle aiming phase available in ordinary play too. No trail shimmer, camera movement or shaking. If reduced motion needs a fully stepped flight, provide a trajectory/landing-position planning version using the same seals and collision rules; validate that version separately because it tests planning more than reflexes. A changed mode is not automatically equally hard.
- Proof/check: use fixed-timestep deterministic physics, capped flight/rally length, nonzero vertical launch velocity and tested collision ordering. Admit only layouts with a replayable winning control trace that respects paddle speed, miss budget and rally limit. For a small prototype, a finite preverified layout bank with seeded mirrors/permutations is safer than an unbounded physics generator. Validate every transformed layout; do not label a finite-bank certificate as a proof about arbitrary layouts. Replays must run through the actual game physics. A tracking baseline should use visible velocity, limited reaction delay and paddle acceleration/speed, not perfect instantaneous interception.

### Game 3: deduction trail — tiny no-guess Minesweeper reference

**[Verified]** [Microsoft's own Minesweeper page](https://zone.msn.com/en/games/msminesweeper) identifies its Windows lineage; [Xbox Game Studios' listing](https://www.xbox.com/en-US/games/store/microsoft-minesweeper/9wzdncrfhwcn) describes logic/strategy, clearing the board without triggering mines, flags and adjustable difficulty. These are publisher descriptions, not evidence of measured enjoyment. [Simon Tatham's creator manual](https://www.chiark.greenend.org.uk/~sgtatham/puzzles/doc/mines.html) explains neighbouring-mine counts and explicitly guarantees deduction without guessing in its default generation mode. That guarantee belongs to **his implementation**, not all Microsoft boards or to our generator by association.

**[Proposal]** One-line prompt: **“Reveal the safe trail—numbers count nearby thorns.”** Begin with a tiny 4×4 board, a safe open patch, two to three hidden thorn tiles, and a creature visibly waiting at the far side. Reveal all safe tiles to complete the trail and catch it; clicking a thorn loses. A number counts thorns in the eight surrounding tiles. Show one clear `1` example in a short visual demo. Flags are optional memory aids, not an additional win requirement. Auto-open zero regions to avoid tedious clicking. This is a reasoning skill game; it is deliberately distinct from the paddle game's motor skill.

- Mouse/touch selects a cell and uses an explicit Reveal/Mark control; keyboard uses arrows, Enter to reveal and F to mark. If direct tap reveals, Mark must be an equally visible toggle; never require long-press or right-click. Keep generous cells, numerals and a selected-cell outline at phone width.
- Rarity adjusts board size (initially 4×4; try 5×5 only after timing), thorn count and the number/depth of deductions needed. Every admitted board still has a no-guess route. More mines alone need not mean harder; classify boards by the deduction solver's required steps/frontier, not only density. A charm can reveal a proven-safe cell with a brief reason. A lure can start with a larger safe opening. Recheck that assistance never removes needed information or increases the task.
- Calm keeps the exact board and decisions, with instant reveals and no running animation. Use a count-up timer for feedback rather than a mandatory reaction deadline; target 15–40 seconds through board size and deduction count. This design cannot promise that every learner finishes under 40 seconds. Report long rounds rather than rush the solver into guesses.
- Proof/check: generate a seeded board/opening, compute its clues, then solve **from those revealed clues**, not by reading hidden thorns. Use a declared small inference set (neighbour counts, known-thorn subtraction and, if needed, subset constraints), recording why each next safe reveal follows. Reject guesses, dead ends, solver timeouts, and boards solved automatically at the opening. Replay the safe-reveal certificate; test clue correctness independently. A complete hidden-layout oracle can verify consistency but cannot establish that a student has enough evidence. Tune a separate bounded deduction baseline, and disclose its reasoning rules.

### Alternative considered: falling-object catch

**[Verified]** The current [Atari × Activision Kaboom page](https://atari.com/products/kaboom-2600-standard-edition) describes paddle-controlled water buckets catching falling bombs. Its praise is sales copy, so this note does not treat “one of the best” as an independent reception finding. **[Proposal]** A 20-second “catch eight falling sparks; miss three and it escapes” reskin would have an exceptionally clear prompt, bounded duration and simple rarity knobs (fall speed, spacing, net width). It is the best substitute if the teacher wants all three games to emphasise immediate motor skill. Prefer the deduction trail initially because falling-object catching overlaps the Breakout paddle's horizontal tracking and creates more simultaneous motion. Do not build a fourth game without a reason from the trio playtest.

### Selection, calibration and acceptance for the trio [proposal]

Choose uniformly among the three games initially, using a dedicated selection RNG stream separate from board/layout generation and outcome simulation. Record the selected game, seed, rarity, item, mode and rules version so a failure can be reproduced. Reveal the game and its one-line prompt before play begins. Keep the first example cheap to retry so random selection does not become a surprise knowledge test. Random selection need not mean a guarantee of no repeats; a shuffle-bag would be a later, explicit product choice.

For each game × rarity × input/mode, report winnability checks and a held-out sensible-policy win rate separately. A 50% overall mixture can hide one game winning 90% and another 10%. Define the baseline before tuning; never add arbitrary bot mistakes merely to hit a number. Pair item/no-item trials on the same seeds and show the practical benefit. The earlier sampling guidance below remains applicable, but bots cannot measure whether students enjoy these games. Famous names, a long Windows history, publisher praise and our own familiarity establish neither “well-reviewed” reception nor a 50% student win rate. No independent historical review score is asserted here.

The teacher/class test should time and score **each** game, record whether the one-line/demo was enough, and ask for a concrete fun/revise/stop verdict for each. Do not let approval of net cuts stand in for approval of all three. Keep all three prototypes small until that verdict, and retain the separate real-device checks for touch, keyboard, phone layout and Calm.

## Two feasible designs [proposal]

### A. Terrain net cuts — recommended first prototype

One-line instruction: **“Close the net around it—watch where it moves.”**

- A small square arena has permanent rocks/hedges and a closed outer boundary. Terrain forms bays and short corridors. Offer a small set of clearly marked horizontal/vertical net lines between solid anchors, rather than arbitrary freehand paths. Highlight the selected line, its growth and the area it could separate.
- One action launches one line. Resolve a fixed number of simulation ticks: the net grows from its anchors, and the creature moves. A creature touching an unfinished section breaks that attempt and spends one allowance. Completed lines remain; shade space disconnected from the creature. Catch when its remaining connected room is at or below a visibly shown target. Losing all allowances or using the turn budget ends the round. Define collisions and event order once, including simultaneous completion/contact.
- Start by trying a 6×6 or 7×7 arena, 8–12 candidate lines, 6–10 actions and two failed-cut allowances. Select one target room size in the prototype. These are feasibility guesses; acceptance requires measurement. At roughly 3–5 seconds per decision the action budget suggests a short round, but only timed playtests can establish duration. Calm should allow extra thinking time rather than impose a 60-second deadline.
- Three deterministic behaviours: **steady** follows a visible direction and reflects at barriers; **turner** follows a short visible rhythm of straight/turn moves; **hunter** aims toward the unfinished net using a fixed, disclosed movement policy. Keep growth, collision and capture rules identical. Show direction/phase/intent with an arrow or small trace; no shake, wobble or jitter. Simplify hunter if explaining its intent requires a paragraph.
- Mouse/touch: tap a marked line to select/preview, then use a large **Cast net** button. Keyboard: arrow through the same lines and Enter/Space to cast. Visible focus and selected-state shapes must not depend only on colour. Require neither dragging nor right-clicking. Check the real phone-width layout.
- Normal animates a committed resolution; Calm/reduced motion steps directly between states with the same result and decisions. No reaction window exists during animation. If later playtests justify real-time play, treat it as a new fairness/accessibility problem, not a visual-only switch.
- An initial automatic, short example shows one successful cut, one danger trace and the shaded-off area. Replays show the broken line or escaped opportunity and the creature path. Lure/charm candidates: one extra failed-cut allowance, or faster growth explicitly shown in the preview. Revalidate affected seeds; faster growth may alter creature behaviour, so improvement is not automatic.

### B. Terrain exit trap — fallback, not a second simultaneous minigame

One-line instruction: **“Block its way out before it reaches the edge.”** A creature advances after each placed blocker; fixed terrain creates a few readable exits and shared choke points. Show intended movement and reachable exits. Win by disconnecting all exits. Three movement policies can share the placement rule. This is the smaller exact-search problem and naturally turn-based, but it risks reproducing the teacher's rejected experience. Use only if a concrete visual prototype makes the new terrain/feedback intelligible and the teacher prefers it. Merely adding rocks to the current interface is not acceptance.

## Proving winnability without hiding luck [proposal]

For A, define a pure finite transition function over terrain, completed lines, creature position/direction/behaviour phase, allowances and turns remaining. Net resolution has fixed ticks and exact cell/edge collision rules. Include every legal player action and every legal wait, if waiting is allowed. Keep the candidate-line set small; memoized exhaustive search can then find a winning sequence or reject the candidate. A search timeout means **unverified**, never “probably winnable.”

Generate deterministically from `(seed, rulesVersion, behaviour, difficulty, item)`. Check initial reachability, legal anchors and that the creature is not already caught. Accept a layout only when the solver produces a legal win within budget. Replay the certificate through the actual game transition function and independently check its final connected room. Bound generation attempts; fall back to a previously verified arena for the same rules/behaviour. Store seed, parameters and winning replay for debugging. Sampling many seeds tests the implementation; the per-layout admission check is what supports “every served layout is solver-winnable.”

**Do not give the solver secret future randomness.** Prefer visible deterministic behaviours initially. For stochastic/hidden future actions, finding one lucky winning path proves only possibility. A guarantee requires a strategy that wins against every permitted creature response, using the information visible at each choice (an AND/OR or belief-state search), or a narrower honestly stated guarantee. For A's three deterministic behaviours, solver and player see the same direction/phase/intent.

Winnable does not mean easy: a perfect solver can win every admitted seed while a limited policy wins half. Conversely, a 50% random coin-flip reward would meet neither the reasoning goal nor the winnability requirement.

## Measuring the proposed 50/50 [proposal; no results yet]

Freeze a transparent **sensible-player baseline** before tuning: inspect only visible state, reject illegal casts, simulate one candidate resolution, prefer safe cuts that reduce the remaining room, then favour shorter lines and break ties with a separate reproducible policy seed. No exhaustive future search, access to the generator's hidden state or silent deliberate losing. Include a cautious baseline and a two-action lookahead baseline to show how sensitive results are to the chosen definition of “sensible.”

Use separate tuning and held-out seed sets, with at least 1,000 held-out layouts per behaviour/difficulty/item/mode where practical. Publish attempts, wins, invalid/rejected/fallback layouts, action counts, policy version and Wilson 95% intervals; around 50% on 1,000 independent seeds has roughly ±3 percentage-point uncertainty. Repeated decisions on one board are not independent trials. Keep solver success separate from baseline success. A provisional 45–55% baseline band for the intended difficulty is a design acceptance band, not an established student rate. Report easier commons and harder rarities separately rather than concealing them in one average.

Compare lure/charm on paired seeds and disclose improved, unchanged and worsened outcomes. Never change the result after the player's actions to force a 50% aggregate. Do not report bot timing as student completion time or simulated “enjoyment.”

Finally, give the teacher the one-arena prototype without extra explanation and record attempts, outcome, time and whether the visible loss made sense. Follow with a small class test, tracking first attempts separately from practice and checking mouse, touch, keyboard and Calm. Ask for an explicit **fun / revise / stop** verdict and concrete reasons. Teacher win within a couple of tries, understandable feedback, sensible session length and desire to replay are separate checks. **The teacher's fun verdict remains a human gate; solver and bot statistics cannot pass it.**

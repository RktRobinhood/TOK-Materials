# Stakes, clocks and one-shot side stories

Research for Rift of Reason (IB TOK maths/logic game, DP1, non-native English speakers). Two story requests from the teacher:

1. A **main story with real stakes**: loved characters in danger (e.g. Granny Axiom slowly lowered into a boiling pot), with **gradient outcomes**, not just win/lose.
2. Many **short one-shot side stories** that pop up (a blinking map icon) at stations already visited, solved by reasoning: negotiation, deduction, spotting a bad argument.

Builds on `research/draw-steel-and-disco-elysium.md` (Draw Steel negotiation and montage, Disco Elysium checks and inner voices). That material is not repeated here; this file links to it where it fits.

Mechanics only: no names, art or text borrowed from commercial games. All summaries are in my own words; sources are given per section.

Status: complete (8 October 2026). Parts 1–4: research summaries with sources. Part 5: adaptation notes (stakes clock, pop-up story template, ten seeds, recommendations).

# Part 1: Gradient stakes and clocks

## 1.1 Blades in the Dark: progress clocks

Source: Blades in the Dark SRD (John Harper, One Seven Design; CC-BY): https://bladesinthedark.com/progress-clocks

- A **clock** is a circle cut into segments. Trouble or effort fills it. When it is full, the thing happens (the alarm sounds, the door gives way).
- **Size = difficulty:** 4 segments for a complex obstacle, 6 for a more complicated one, 8 for a daunting one.
- **Kinds of clock** worth stealing:
  - **Danger clock:** counts up to something bad (guards notice, the pot boils). A small consequence fills 1 segment, a big one 2 or 3.
  - **Racing clocks:** two clocks at once, e.g. *escape* vs *capture*. Whichever fills first wins. This is the cleanest picture of "you vs the villain".
  - **Linked clocks:** filling one opens the next (break the lock, then the vault).
  - **Tug-of-war clock:** events can fill *or empty* it. This is the shape for a negotiation or a mood.
  - **Mission clock:** a deadline; when it fills, the chance is gone or changes.
  - **Faction clocks:** NPC groups advance their plans between missions, whatever the players do.
- Advice from the text: name the clock after the obstacle, not the method ("The Tower", not "Climb the Tower"); use a clock only for layered situations; a clock *shows* progress, it does not decide it; split a big threat into several small clocks.

## 1.2 Blades in the Dark: position and effect

Source: https://bladesinthedark.com/action-roll

- Before a roll the GM says the **position** (how dangerous: controlled, risky, desperate) and the **effect** (how much you can achieve: limited, standard, great). Both are said out loud, so the player knows the stakes before acting.
- Results have three bands: full success; **success with a consequence** (harm, a complication, less effect, or a worse position); and things going badly. A critical gives extra effect.
- The worse the position, the harsher the consequence in each band. The same 4–5 result means "a small snag" when controlled and "serious harm" when desperate.
- **Lesson for us:** two separate dials. *Position* = how fast the danger clock fills if you get it wrong. *Effect* = how many segments of the good clock a right answer fills. Showing both before the student clicks is what makes a gradient outcome feel fair.

## 1.3 Powered by the Apocalypse: partial success, soft and hard moves

Sources: Dungeon World SRD (Sage LaTorra and Adam Koebel, CC-BY), gamemastering: https://www.dungeonworldsrd.com/gamemastering/ and fronts: https://www.dungeonworldsrd.com/gamemastering/fronts/ ; The Daily Apocalypse (fan blog on Apocalypse World) on countdown clocks: https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/85-threat-countdown-clocks

- **Three bands on every roll:** 10+ you get what you want; **7–9 you get it with a cost, a hard choice or a complication**; 6 or less the GM makes a move. The middle band is the heart of PbtA: most results are mixed, so most scenes move.
- **Soft vs hard moves:** a soft move is a warning with time to react ("you hear the water start to bubble"); a hard move has an immediate, permanent consequence. An ignored soft move earns a hard move. This is a fairness rule: **always warn before you hurt**.
- Useful GM moves for a puzzle game: *show signs of an approaching threat*; *offer an opportunity, with or without a cost*; *tell them the requirements or consequences and ask*; *reveal an unwelcome truth*; *put someone in a spot*.
- **Fronts:** secret notes listing 2–3 dangers. Each danger has an **impulse** (what it wants), **grim portents** (1–3 steps for a short adventure, 3–5 for a campaign) leading to an **impending doom**, and 1–3 **stakes questions** whose answers nobody decides in advance ("Will the baker survive?"). Portents tick when play causes them or the GM uses one as a hard move. When all have happened, the world changes.
- **Apocalypse World countdown clocks:** the threat clock runs like a clock face; the early segments are big and slow, the last three are small and fast, so you can *see* when things get serious. It reminds the GM that threats act on their own momentum.

## 1.4 Fail forward

Sources: Gnome Stew, "Failing Forward": https://gnomestew.com/failing-forward-how-to-make-failure-interesting-in-rpgs/ ; Daily Apocalypse on why "fail forward" mis-describes AW moves: https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/143-beyond-success-failure-failing-forward-and-moves-in-apocalypse-world

- Idea: a failure should change the situation, not stop the game or just invite "try again". The term is informal (13th Age's "no whiffing" and PbtA systems made it mainstream).
- Menu of fail-forward results (Gnome Stew): cut off or redirected; a penalty; a lost or stuck item; an extra challenge; overshoot (too much success causes a new problem); counterbalance (you gain, they gain); a condition (a funny status like "soggy"); a doom clock ticking.
- Pitfalls: the same consequence every time becomes dull; **don't take away resources the player chose to keep, let them choose to spend**; not every failure needs a twist (some low-stakes checks can just fail); and prepare a menu of consequences in advance.

## 1.5 Ticking clocks in video games

- **Majora's Mask (Nintendo, 2000).** The world ends in three days; the player rewinds and keeps knowledge. NPCs follow fixed schedules, tracked in a notebook; one side story (a couple separated before a wedding) takes a whole cycle and has timed meetings and a slow-but-still-works route. The director found a planned one-week loop too long to remember who was where, so it was cut to three days. Sources: Zelda Universe on the original week-long plan (citing Iwata Asks): https://zeldauniverse.net/2019/05/07/zeldas-study-majoras-mask-was-originally-planned-to-have-a-weeklong-cycle/ ; Zelda Wiki on the side quest: https://zeldawiki.wiki/wiki/Anju%27s_Anguish
  - *Steal:* a short, readable deadline; the rewind as forgiveness; knowledge kept across tries (perfect for our time rifts: "the student remembers what the villain said last loop").
- **Pentiment (Obsidian, 2022).** A murder investigation where an official is on his way: each lead you follow costs time, so you can't follow every lead. You accuse someone with incomplete evidence, and the game never confirms the real killer. The director says he designed it so there could not be one right answer. Sources: Shacknews: https://www.shacknews.com/article/146276/pentiment-josh-sawyer-no-correct-answer ; Twinfinite review: https://twinfinite.net/2022/11/pentiment-review-a-medieval-murder-mystery-to-die-for/
  - *Steal:* time measured in **actions**, not seconds (good for non-native speakers who read slowly); "you could not check everything" as a TOK point about justification under uncertainty. *Don't steal:* the unresolved ending; our side stories should have a checkable answer.
- **FTL: Faster Than Light (Subset Games, 2012).** A pursuing fleet advances one step each time you jump: a map-wide clock that pushes you on. Text events offer choices; special ("blue") options appear only if you have the right crew or kit, and are safe or better. Sources: Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FTL:_Faster_Than_Light ; GDC postmortem "Designing Without a Pitch": https://gdcvault.com/play/1019036/Designing-Without-a-Pitch-FTL
  - *Steal:* the blue option. A side story can show an extra choice only because of the avatar, a caught creature, an item or an earlier story flag. It rewards collecting without being needed.

# Part 2: One-shot and side-quest structure

## 2.1 Justin Alexander: the Three Clue Rule and node-based design

Sources (The Alexandrian, the designer's own blog): Three Clue Rule: https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule ; Node-Based Scenario Design part 1: https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/7949/roleplaying-games/node-based-scenario-design-part-1-the-plot-based-scenario ; part 3, inverting the rule: https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/7956/roleplaying-games/node-based-scenario-design-part-3-inverting-the-three-clue-rule

- **Problem:** a mystery stalls when one clue is a chokepoint: the player must find it, notice it, understand it and draw the right conclusion. Any of those can fail.
- **Rule:** for every conclusion the players must reach, plant **at least three clues**. Think of each clue as a plan with two backups. Any problem the adventure can't continue without should also have **at least three solutions**.
- **Be permissive:** if a player reasons well by a route you didn't plan, reward it. The prepared clues are a safety net.
- **Proactive clues:** if the players are stuck, make something happen (the villain acts, someone new arrives), ideally bringing a new clue.
- **Red herrings are risky:** players invent their own wrong suspects; designers rarely need to add more.
- **Write the revelations as a checklist** (conclusion + the clues that support it) and tick a conclusion only when the players actually reach it.
- **Node-based design:** instead of a fixed chain A→B→C (each arrow a chokepoint), make **nodes** (places, people, events) that each hold clues pointing to other nodes. Players choose the order. **Inverted rule:** if players can reach three clues, they will usually reach at least one conclusion, so the scenario keeps moving even when some clues are missed. Prep situations, not plots.
- **For a puzzle game:** in a 5–10 minute one-shot the "three clues" can be three things to click (a witness, an object, a sign), any two of which are enough to solve it. That keeps it short and forgiving.

## 2.2 Johnn Four: the Five Room Dungeon

Source (the designer's own site): https://www.roleplayingtips.com/5-room-dungeons/

Five beats, not literal rooms; the shape works for a heist, a negotiation or a trial.

1. **Entrance and guardian:** an early challenge that sets the mood and explains why the problem hasn't been solved already.
2. **Puzzle or roleplay challenge:** a change of pace; allow several solutions; plant its clues in beat 1.
3. **Trick or setback:** a false win or a reversal that raises tension (the treasure room is fake).
4. **Climax:** the big confrontation.
5. **Reward, revelation or twist:** the payoff, plus a hook to something later. This beat is what players remember.

## 2.3 Sly Flourish (Mike Shea): the Lazy DM's eight steps

Source (the designer's own blog): https://slyflourish.com/eight_steps_2023.html ; free CC-BY Lazy GM's Resource Document: https://slyflourish.com/lazy_gm_resource_document.html

The eight steps: review the characters, **strong start**, outline potential scenes, **secrets and clues**, **fantastic locations**, important NPCs, monsters, rewards. The three most relevant:

- **Strong start:** pull players straight in with "something happens", which usually leads to the hook. No slow set-up.
- **Secrets and clues:** the heart of the method. About ten one- or two-sentence facts the players might learn, relevant to what they want to know. Write the *fact*, not where it is found; decide the route (an NPC, a mural, a note) during play. Unrevealed secrets carry over to the next session.
- **Fantastic locations:** a sentence each; for key places, one to three striking features players can *use*. For us: every station already has a painted scene; a side story should add one interactive feature to it (the stall's prize shelf, the bridge's lever).
- Overall: the steps are a menu; keep what helps.

## 2.4 One-shot pacing

Sources: Ron Gilbert, "Why Adventure Games Suck" (1989, designer's own blog): https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck/ ; plus the three sources above.

- **Know the goal at once, and the first step** (Gilbert). A one-shot is too short for wandering.
- **No deaths or dead ends a careful player can't avoid** (Gilbert). Danger is fine if cleverness gets you out.
- **Problem before solution:** show the locked door before the key, so finding the key feels like a solution (Gilbert's "backwards puzzles").
- **Reward intent:** if the player is clearly trying the right thing, accept it.
- **Real-time clocks make poor drama; use "Hollywood time":** give slack and let near-misses squeak through. Gilbert aims for roughly 10% early, 10% fail, 80% succeed at the last moment. This is exactly how a stakes clock should be tuned.
- Combined rule of thumb for 5–10 minutes: strong start (30 s), 3 clues in any order (3–4 min), one twist (1 min), a decision with gradient results (1–2 min), payoff (30 s).

## 2.5 Side quests in video games

- **The Witcher 3 (CD Projekt Red, 2015).** A studio rule of "no fetch quests": every side quest tells a story, often turning partway (a simple job reveals a family tragedy; a monster contract turns out to be about a person). The lead quest designer admitted a few still felt padded. The best-known one starts as "find a missing person" and becomes a domestic drama with outcomes that range from bad to worse, shaped by earlier choices. Sources: Stevivor interview with Mateusz Tomaszkiewicz: https://stevivor.com/features/interviews/cd-projekt-reds-mateusz-tomaszkiewicz-on-the-witcher-3-wild-hunt-and-lessons-learned-for-hearts-of-stone/ ; mein-mmo report on the no-fetch rule (German): https://mein-mmo.de/witcher-4-keine-fetch-quests/
  - *Steal:* **the twist reframes the request.** The person who asked for help is part of the problem, or the "monster" has a reason. In a TOK game the twist can be epistemic: "the evidence you trusted was the biased part".
- **Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, 2019).** Tasks are split into a few essential ones and many optional ones; most are side tasks. Small cases include investigating a shop space where every business fails (locals believe it is cursed) and restocking a trap for cryptid hunters. Each ends in a small revelation, often funny, sometimes sad, and every task gives XP. Source: https://discoelysium.wiki.gg/wiki/Tasks
  - *Steal:* small local mysteries with a **believable explanation that beats the superstitious one**: a perfect fit for confirmation bias and induction in chapter 1.
- **Ace Attorney (Capcom, 2001 onward).** The creator's first prototype asked players to find one contradiction across a whole trial; testers got lost. He split trials into small chunks with an "aha" at each, so players can still reason towards an answer even if they missed a piece of evidence. Core loop: hear the full testimony, then cross-examine, then present the item that contradicts a line. Sources: Siliconera on Shu Takumi and Iwata Asks: https://www.siliconera.com/2015/04/27/shu-takumi-provides-a-closer-look-at-the-great-ace-attorney/ ; Nintendo Everything on the prototype: https://nintendoeverything.com/ace-attorney-creator-on-initial-prototype-and-nearly-suspending-development-maya-and-mias-origins-more/
  - *Steal:* **one contradiction per small chunk**, never one giant leap. Already planned for chapter 3; side stories can reuse the same press/present UI in a 3-line mini version.
- **Professor Layton (Level-5, 2007 onward).** Built from Akira Tago's puzzle books; the team wrote puzzles first and story second, and the director admitted early puzzles were often unrelated to the plot, so the sequel tried to tie them in. Townspeople hand you puzzles constantly: the template for a "puzzle per NPC" side layer. Sources: Game Informer, "Figuring out Professor Layton's tricks": https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2010/09/24/figuring-out-professor-layton-s-tricks.aspx ; Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layton%27s_Mystery_Journey
  - *Steal:* a **stock of small classic puzzles wrapped in one-line NPC excuses**; *avoid* Layton's weakness by making the puzzle the way the story problem is solved, not a toll before it.

# Part 3: Hostage and crisis negotiation as a game

## 3.1 The FBI Behavioral Change Stairway Model

Sources: Wikipedia, Crisis negotiation: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_negotiation ; van der Klok, Oostinga, Russell and Yansick (an FBI supervisory agent), "Accelerating influence", CREST Security Review, Winter 2024: https://crestresearch.ac.uk/download/4897/csr18_van_der_klok_p8-9.pdf ; Vecchi, Van Hasselt and Romano (2005), "Crisis (hostage) negotiation: current strategies and issues", abstract: https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/crisis-hostage-negotiation-current-strategies-and-issues-high-risk ; Black Swan Group on the FBI's active-listening skill list: https://www.blackswanltd.com/the-edge/just-talk-to-him

- Built by the FBI's Crisis Negotiation Unit. Five steps climbed in order: **active listening → empathy → rapport → influence → behavioural change** (e.g. the person lets the hostages go or comes out). The key rule: don't push for the change before rapport exists.
- A revised version (for people in suicidal crisis) adds **trust** (being seen as honest and able to keep promises) between rapport and influence. Active listening runs underneath every step as the "handrail".
- **Active listening skills** (the FBI's training list): minimal encouragers ("I see", "OK"), open questions, mirroring (repeat the last few words), **emotion labelling** ("You sound let down"), paraphrasing, "I" messages, effective pauses, summarising. Trainers warn that using them in a fixed order sounds robotic and harms trust.
- **Critique worth teaching:** the stairway assumes each step must be done before the next. A survey of 84 negotiators in 14 countries found 78% had sometimes skipped ahead, and an experiment found a direct early request worked in about a third of cases without harming the relationship. The authors suggest a **climbing wall** metaphor: several routes, shortcuts where possible. (A nice TOK point in itself: a model is a simplification.)

## 3.2 How games have used negotiation

- **Draw Steel** (Interest and Patience tracks, hidden motivations and pitfalls, a 0–5 offer table): fully covered in `draw-steel-and-disco-elysium.md` §1.1 and adapted there in §3.1. Use that as the engine.
- **Persona 5 (Atlus, 2016) demon talks.** You must first knock the enemy down (leverage), then talk. Each demon has one of four moods (upbeat, timid, irritable, gloomy) and likes one style of answer (funny, kind, serious, vague). The mood is shown on screen, and the question hints at the right tone. A wrong answer angers it and the fight resumes. Source: RPG Site guide: https://www.rpgsite.net/feature/13327-persona-5-royal-demon-negotiation-answers-hold-up-guide
  - *Lesson:* a **visible personality label** makes the "read the person" step easy for beginners; the challenge is matching tone, not guessing a hidden rule.
- **Griftlands (Klei, 2021).** Negotiation is a second card battle: you lower the opponent's resolve while protecting yours; their side has separate "arguments" with their own health that you can attack or ignore. Losing a negotiation doesn't end the run, it changes what happens next (often a fight). Sources: Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griftlands ; Game Informer review: https://gameinformer.com/review/griftlands/griftlands-review-great-friends-in-low-places
  - *Lesson:* the opponent's **arguments as targets** maps well onto "break down their argument": knock out the weak premise and the conclusion falls. Reviewers found two parallel systems hard to learn, so keep ours to one simple screen.
- **Disco Elysium:** talk is the whole game; failure is meant to be interesting. Covered in the earlier file, §2.
- **Monkey Island (LucasArts, 1990) insult sword fighting.** Duels are won by answering each insult with the matching comeback; you learn new insults and comebacks by losing to pirates, then use them on the champion, who uses new insults you must match by meaning. Source: Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Secret_of_Monkey_Island
  - *Lesson:* **learning by losing, then applying it to a new case** is a perfect fallacy-spotting loop: collect "bad argument → the right reply" pairs, then face a boss who uses new wordings of the same fallacies.

## 3.3 What makes a negotiation scene fun and fair

Drawn from all the above:

1. **The other side must be torn** (Draw Steel): they have a reason to agree and a reason to refuse. A criminal with hostages wants something; find out what.
2. **Readable feedback after every line**: a face, a bar, a one-word reaction. Persona shows the mood; Draw Steel signals positive, negative or impatient.
3. **Information is a resource**: asking or observing costs a little (Patience, a turn) but makes the next choice better.
4. **Order matters, but not rigidly**: listen before you ask (the stairway), with a rare, risky shortcut for bold players (the climbing wall).
5. **A clear set of end states, most of them partial** (Draw Steel's "Yes, and" to "No, and"). Losing changes the route; it never blocks the game.
6. **Never more than 3–5 choices per turn**, each a short sentence.
7. **Threats are not negotiation** (Draw Steel; and the stairway). In our game, the "threaten" option should exist and always make things worse: a cheap, funny lesson.

# Part 4: Stakes that land with teens without being grim

Sources: Ron Gilbert's essay above; Monkey Island (Wikipedia, above); Kill Screen, "Behind the humor of Toby Fox's Undertale": https://killscreen.com/behind-humor-toby-foxs-undertale ; Paper Mario: The Thousand-Year Door (Wikipedia): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_Mario:_The_Thousand-Year_Door

- **Monkey Island.** The love interest is kidnapped by a ghost pirate and a forced wedding looms: real stakes. But the hero almost can't die (a fall off a cliff bounces him back up, parodying other games' game-over screens), and the villain is beaten with fizzy root beer. Gilbert called punishing tiny mistakes a cheap way out for designers. *Peril in the plot, never in the controls.*
- **Paper Mario: The Thousand-Year Door.** Between chapters you play the kidnapped princess inside the villains' base, where she befriends the base's computer and teaches it about love in exchange for a way to email the hero. You also play the clumsy rival villain chasing the same goal. *The captive gets agency and comedy, and the player sees the danger from the inside*, which makes it feel real without being cruel.
- **Undertale.** First make players fond of the characters (simple art, a silent hero), then put them under tension, then undercut it with a goofy joke; scary monsters turn out to be merely hungry. Kill Screen calls it befriending the player first. The creator cites a game that is "funny and strange but also sincere" as his model (paraphrased).

**Rules we can take:**

1. **Make them loved first.** Granny Axiom must have funny, kind moments (the teaching match, dry jokes) *before* she is ever in the pot.
2. **The danger is absurd, the feeling is real.** A tortoise lowered into soup on a pulley with a "SOUP OF THE DAY" sign is silly; her calm, dry jokes while dangling are sweet; the player's wish to save her is real.
3. **Show it slowly and always warn** (PbtA soft moves). Each wrong answer lowers the rope one notch with a sound cue and a funny line. No sudden surprises.
4. **Nobody dies in a side story; the worst outcome is embarrassing, costly or sad-funny** (Granny rescued but grumpy and soggy, her shawl lost; the villain escapes with the prize). Save true loss for the story's own big beats, and even then make it reversible through the time rift.
5. **Give captives a voice** (Paper Mario): Granny gives clues from the pot; hostages can be talked to.
6. **Villains are pompous, not cruel** (Monkey Island, Paper Mario's bumbling rival). The Algorithm's minions lower the pot *because the feed demanded more engagement*, with live view counts. The cruelty belongs to the system, which is the TOK point.
7. **Undercut, then return to sincerity.** A joke after each tense beat, but let the rescue moment land with one honest line.

# Part 5: Adaptation notes for Rift of Reason

Design rules used throughout (from DESIGN.md, AGENTS.md and the earlier research file): mouse only; short plain English (a line is at most about 15 words); no dead ends, a bad result changes the route; honest numbers on screen; no looping shake or wobble (the pot lowers in calm steps); caricatures joke only about public personas. Side stories are hand-written scenes that can embed a small generated puzzle, so they stay replayable and hard to paste into a chatbot.

## 5.1 (a) The stakes clock

One reusable widget for main-story boss chapters and side stories. It is Blades' **racing clocks** (you vs the danger) with PbtA's **soft-move warnings** and Gilbert's **Hollywood time**.

### What is on screen

- **The Danger clock** (red, 6 notches by default; 4 for an easy side story, 8 for a boss). It is drawn *as the fiction*: a rope with notches beside the pot, a candle fuse, a crowd mood meter, a sandglass. A small pie in the corner repeats it for clarity. As in Apocalypse World, the **last two notches are drawn smaller and redder**, so students can see when it gets serious.
- **The Progress clock** (gold, 3–6 notches): the solution building up (rope pulled back, the hostage-taker's trust, the case against the culprit).
- **Position and effect tags on choices** (Blades): a small icon on each button. A **flame** = risky (a wrong answer costs 2 notches); no flame = safe (costs 1). A **star** = big effect (a right answer fills 2 progress). The student sees the stakes before clicking.
- **A one-line warning at every tick** (soft move), voiced, with a sound cue: "The rope creaks. Granny: 'Mind the paintwork, dear.'" Never a hard consequence without a warning first.

### What fills the Danger clock (bad)

| Event | Notches |
|---|---|
| A wrong accusation, wrong check, or wrong "present" | 1 (safe) or 2 (risky, flame) |
| Choosing a "Can't stand" argument in a negotiation, or a threat | 1 |
| Every action after a free budget (e.g. the 4th, 5th... clue click in a side story) | 1 per extra action (Pentiment-style time in actions, not seconds) |
| Ignoring a warning that named a fix ("The rope is fraying, find something to tie it") | 1 extra |

**No real-time timer.** Time is counted in actions and mistakes, which suits slow readers and Calm motion.

### What drains Danger or fills Progress (good)

| Event | Effect |
|---|---|
| A right deduction or check | +1 progress (+2 with a star) |
| A correct "Why?" answer after it | +1 progress, or drain 1 danger (player chooses: a tiny risk/benefit decision) |
| **Stall** actions: active listening lines (mirror, label the feeling, summarise) | drain 1 danger, once each per scene |
| A **blue option** (FTL): a line only shown because of the avatar, a matching-colour creature on the team, an item, or an earlier story flag | drain 1 danger or +1 progress |
| Spending a catch charm, a hint token or a heart (the player's choice) | drain 1 danger |

**Hearts and the clock don't double-punish.** Inside a stakes scene, mistakes tick the clock instead of costing hearts (the free-check rules do not apply). Hearts only come in when the *player chooses* to spend one to buy back a notch, following Gnome Stew's advice to let players spend resources rather than lose them.

### The four outcome tiers

The scene ends when Progress fills. The tier depends on how full Danger is at that moment. If Danger fills first, it is tier 4.

| Tier | Danger when solved | Name | What it means (Draw Steel / PbtA band) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–2 of 6 | **Clean save** | "Yes, and": the goal plus a bonus (a creature joins, a rare item, a funny thank-you) |
| 2 | 3–4 | **Close call** | "Yes, but": the goal, with a small visible cost (Granny is soggy and grumpy; the stall's sign is broken) |
| 3 | 5 | **Saved, at a price** | 7–9 with a bite: the goal, but the villain escapes with something, or a station closes for one visit, or a team creature is scarred (uses the existing fate table's scarred state, never death) |
| 4 | Danger fills | **Too late, for now** | Fail forward: the bad thing happens *absurdly* (Granny is served as soup... and complains that the soup is under-seasoned). The story continues on a harder branch, and a **time-rift retry** or a later rescue node opens. Never a game over. |

**Tuning target (Gilbert's 10/80/10):** about 25% of players reach tier 1, most reach tier 2, about 10% reach tier 3, and about 5% tier 4. Tune the notch counts with the same simulator habit used for battles: simulate a "careful", "average" and "guessing" player.

### What carries forward

Each scene saves `stakes.<sceneId> = 1–4`. Use it in three ways, from cheapest to richest:

1. **Reactions**: NPCs and the Sundial mention the outcome later ("You saved my shawl. I knitted you a scarf." / "I still smell of soup."). One or two lines each; cheap and very effective.
2. **Small world changes**: a stall closed for one visit, a shop discount, a cosmetic (Granny's knitted scarf), a creature available or not, a rumour opened.
3. **The Feed clock (campaign front).** One hidden-then-revealed 8-notch clock for The Algorithm across the whole game (Dungeon World's campaign front with grim portents; Blades' faction clock). Tier 3 results tick it +1, tier 4 +2, tier 1 drains 1. Each chapter ends with a grim portent shown on the map (the sky eye grows a little; more feed-screens). In the finale, the Algorithm's core starts with that many extra shields, but cap it (at most +3) so nobody is locked out. In lesson 4 the reveal shows the Feed clock as **a probability**: "You gave me 6 out of 8. I predicted you would." The clock that measured the danger becomes the villain's own statistics, which is the story's TOK point.

### Main-story use: who is in danger, chapter by chapter (suggestions)

Make each one loved first (Undertale): give them a funny, kind scene before the danger.

A live view counter beside the pot rises with every notch: the imps lower her because the feed rewards drama.

## 5.2 (b) The one-shot template: "Pop-up stories"

A pop-up story appears at a station the player has already visited, shown by a **softly glowing speech-bubble icon** on the map (a slow fade in and out, not a bounce or a shake; Calm motion makes it static). Hover gives a one-line teaser. At most **two** are shown at once, so the map stays calm. They don't expire (no Majora's Mask stress), but a new one only appears after the player completes a lesson or solves N more stations, so they feel like a reward for coming back.

### Shape: 5–10 minutes, six beats

| Beat | Time | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Strong start** | 30 s | Something is already happening when you arrive. 2–3 voiced lines state the goal and show the stakes clock. | Lazy DM; Gilbert (goal and first step clear) |
| 2. **Three clues** | 3–4 min | Three glowing hotspots on the station's existing background: usually **a person** (a short statement), **an object** (a close-up), and **a record** (a sign, ledger, chart). Any order. **Any two are enough** to solve. Each becomes a clue card in a small notebook strip. The inner voice may comment on one. | Three Clue Rule; node-based design; Lazy DM's secrets |
| 3. **The twist** | 1 min | A new fact reframes the request: the asker is part of the problem, the evidence is a biased sample, the "hostage-taker" is also a victim of the feed. It adds one clue card and can change the goal. | Witcher 3; Five Room Dungeon setback |
| 4. **Resolution by reasoning** | 1–3 min | One of four **verbs**, each reusing an engine the game has or plans: **Deduce** (click the culprit, the true statement, or the safe door, then a one-click "Why?"); **Negotiate** (the Draw Steel-lite screen from the earlier file, 3–4 turns, with the stairway: listen before you ask); **Object** (a mini cross-examination: 3–4 lines, press or present one clue card); **Test** (choose the test that could prove the claim wrong, as in Rule Hunter). | Ace Attorney in small chunks; Draw Steel; FBI stairway |
| 5. **Gradient outcome** | 30 s | The stakes clock decides tiers 1–4. A short scene for each tier. | §5.1 |
| 6. **Reward** | 30 s | XP and a charm for every tier; tier 1–2 adds a bonus (a creature visitor with better catch odds, a cosmetic, a rumour, an argument card); a story flag a later NPC mentions. | Lazy DM rewards; Five Room Dungeon revelation |

### Rules for writers

- **Text budget:** about 25 lines in total, one idea per line, at most 15 words. Three to five choices per turn.
- **Problem before solution** (Gilbert): show the locked tent before the key.
- **Accept good reasoning by another route** (the permissive rule): if two different clue pairs prove it, both work.
- **Use the station's own cast and background**, plus at most one new prop. No new backgrounds per story.
- **Every story ends with a one-line TOK reflection from the Sundial** (as bosses already do), optional to read.
- **One blue option per story** (avatar, creature colour, item or earlier flag).
- **Nobody dies; nothing permanent is lost in a side story.** The worst outcome is funny, costly or embarrassing.
- **Replayable seed:** the clue contents (which hostage is lying, which number is missing) are drawn from a small table with the game's seeded RNG.

Most appear at **earlier stations after a later lesson**, so students go back home with new tools (the teacher's "criminal at the Fair" idea). Each uses the template above: 3 clues (any two enough), one twist, a reasoning verb, 4 tiers. Tiers are written as 1 Clean save / 2 Close call / 3 Saved at a price / 4 Too late, for now.

### 1. Siege at the Witness Tent (Fair, after lesson 1)

- **Hook:** A pickpocket imp has barricaded himself in Madame Mirage's tent with three fairgoers (Keanu Meows, Mr. Beansprout, a Speedcheeta cub) and her crystal ball. Constable Clobber wants to charge in. Everyone "knows" he stole the Fair trophy: a clip on the feed shows it.
- **Clock:** Danger = Clobber's patience (6). Progress = the imp's trust (4).
- **Clues:** *Person:* Mirage: "The clip shows his paw near the trophy." *Object:* the clip, zoomed out: it was cropped; just outside the frame, Siuuugull's celebrating wing knocks the trophy off its stand. *Record:* the lost-and-found book: the trophy was handed in an hour ago.
- **Reasoning skill:** confirmation bias (everyone saw what they expected), and the negotiation stairway: each reply is tagged **Listen / Feel / Trust / Ask**. Asking him to come out before trust is built costs a notch. Labelling his feeling ("You're scared nobody will believe you") drains one.
- **Twist:** he didn't steal the trophy, but Keanu kindly points out that he *did* pick Clobber's pocket on the way in. Innocent of one claim, guilty of another: judge each claim on its evidence.
- **Blue option:** Moth-kin: "Look at the edge of the picture."
- **Tiers:** 1: everyone walks out, the trophy is found, he returns the wallet and becomes a friendly trainer who gives a lure. 2: everyone out, but the tent curtain is torn; Mirage's stall is closed for one visit. 3: Clobber charges in, everyone is covered in fortune-teller glitter, the imp escapes (a rumour about him appears). 4: he escapes with the crystal ball; Mirage reads fortunes from a teacup until seed 9 or a later pop-up brings it back.
- **Sundial:** "Everyone saw it. Did anyone check it?"

### 2. The Wishing Well's Winning Streak (Road, after lesson 1)

- **Hook:** Siuuugull tells a queue of villagers the well works: he wished before matches and scored. Chimpossible is livestreaming ("entirely possible!"). Villagers are throwing in their savings, including Mrs Crumb's flour money.
- **Clock:** Danger = coins lost (6). Progress = villagers who stop (4).
- **Clues:** *Person:* Siuuugull's list of matches: only the ones where he scored. *Record:* the referee's full log: he scored in 6 of 10 matches after wishing, and in 6 of 10 when he forgot. *Object:* at the bottom of the well, a masked imp scooping up coins.
- **Reasoning skill:** induction from a one-sided sample; ask for the misses; pick the test that could prove the well wrong.
- **Twist:** the well-keeper is an imp collecting "engagement donations" for the Algorithm.
- **Resolution (Test):** "Which data would show whether wishing works?" Four buttons: his scoring matches / all matches he wished for / all matches, with and without wishing / what the stream's viewers think. Then read a 2×2 table (generated numbers each play).
- **Blue option:** Frogling: "Remember the matches he forgot to wish?"
- **Tiers:** 1: coins returned; Siuuugull laughs it off and appears as a catchable visitor; Chimpossible asks to "pull up the full table". 2: most coins returned. 3: the imp escapes with half; bakery prices rise for one visit. 4: the imp escapes with all of it; the well becomes a rumour spot to revisit.
- **Sundial:** "A pattern you only count when it works is not a pattern."

### 3. Two Black Cats (Road, after lesson 1)

- **Hook:** Rawmsay's bake-off pie has vanished. He is furious and will shut the Campfire kitchen for everyone. Villagers blame Keanu Meows: "Every time a pie goes missing, the black cat walks by."
- **Clock:** Danger = Rawmsay's temper (6). Progress = the case (3).
- **Clues:** *Person:* Sir David Attenbirdough whispers that he saw the black cat pass twice, a minute apart, in the same direction. *Object:* paw prints in two sizes. *Record:* Keanu's signed scorecard: he was at Syllo's gallery at noon.
- **Reasoning skill:** induction ("every time...") suggests; deduction proves. A small elimination grid (3 suspects × 3 clues).
- **Twist:** there are two black cats; the second is an imp in a cat mask (a foreshadowing of the masked imps in chapter 2). Keanu's famous "déjà vu" was real.
- **Tiers:** 1: the pie is recovered (half-eaten); Rawmsay switches from fury to tender kindness, and Keanu joins as a visitor. 2: the pie is gone, but the kitchen stays open. 3: Keanu is cleared, but the kitchen closes for a visit. 4: Keanu is wrongly sent away down the Road; you meet him at a later station, where he forgives you (he is famously nice). A gentle sting: punishing on a pattern can hurt the innocent.
- **Sundial:** "Seeing something every time is not the same as knowing why."

### 4. The Three Cake Tins (Boolesbury Bakery, after lesson 2)

- **Hook:** Mrs Crumb baked the cake for the Mayor's feast and hid it in one of three tins. Imps have lit the oven under all three. One holds the cake, one a custard trap, one the Mayor's itching powder.
- **Clock:** Danger = oven heat (6; a wrong tin costs 2). Progress = 3.
- **Clues:** *Record:* the three labels (generated, e.g. A: "The cake is here." B: "The cake is not here." C: "The cake is not in A."). *Person:* Mrs Crumb: "I wrote them so at most one label tells the truth." *Object:* Pip the Clerk's delivery note shows which tin was swapped last (narrows the cases).
- **Reasoning skill:** a three-row truth table ("Suppose the cake is in A: which labels are true? Is that allowed?"). Uses a miniature of the chapter 2 truth-table tool. Public-domain puzzle shape (the caskets in *The Merchant of Venice*, in Smullyan's logic-puzzle style).
- **Twist:** the cake has a message from Granny Axiom baked inside: "Bring a ladle." The feast is the soup feast of the chapter 2 boss.
- **Main-story link:** tier 1 or 2 here gives a ladle that **drains 1 notch** from the Granny pot clock.
- **Tiers:** 1: the cake is saved and the ladle found. 2: the cake is saved, slightly singed; ladle found. 3: custard trap: the avatar is "Custardy" for one visit (cosmetic); no ladle. 4: the cake burns; Mrs Crumb bakes another, no note.
- **Sundial:** "Every statement is true or false. The work is finding out which."

### 5. The Silent Pupil (Boolesbury Schoolhouse, after lesson 2)

- **Hook:** Someone chalked "THE MAYOR IS AN IMP" on the blackboard. Headmaster Lobstorian will lecture the whole class through the Fair holiday unless the writer is found. Four pupils: Astrophysicat, Khaby Llame, Billie Eelish, Mr. Beansprout.
- **Clock:** Danger = the lecture's length (each wrong accusation adds "ten more minutes"; 6). Progress = 3.
- **Clues:** *Person:* statements (generated): the writer lies, the others tell the truth. Astrophysicat: "Well, actually, Khaby did it." Khaby says nothing but points at chalk on Astrophysicat's paw (a gesture, not a statement). Billie whispers; you hear half unless you click "listen closer" (costs an action). Beansprout makes no statement at all. *Object:* chalk dust. *Record:* the seating chart.
- **Reasoning skill:** mark each pupil Truth/Lie and find the only consistent row. Also: **someone who makes no statement is neither a liar nor proven honest**; silence is not evidence.
- **Twist:** the message is *true* (the Mayor is the Arch-Imp). The writer was being brave. Final choice: hand them in, or tell Lobstorian the message is evidence.
- **Tiers:** 1: the writer is found and protected; Lobstorian, persuaded, gives a two-minute lecture instead of forty, and a Reason-colour visitor appears. 2: found, but Lobstorian lectures for an hour (comic scene). 3: wrong pupil scolded first, then cleared. 4: the whole class gets the full lecture; Lobstorian is extra grumpy as a gate guard for one visit.
- **Sundial:** "Silence is not a lie. But it is not proof either."

### 6. Syllo's Recruitment Drive (Fair, after lesson 3)

- **Hook:** Sergeant Syllo has drafted half the Fair into his toy army. At the end of the drum roll they march into the Rift.
- **Clock:** Danger = the drum (6). Progress = recruits freed (4).
- **Clues:** *Record:* his posters: "Everyone's joining!" (popularity), "March or be a coward!" (false choice), "The Rockodile says so!" (authority, with the eyebrow). *Person:* Usain Volt: "I joined because everyone else did." *Object:* Syllo's own recruitment letter, sent by the feed.
- **Reasoning skill:** name the fallacy and pick the reply that answers it (the insult-duel loop from Monkey Island: you learn replies by losing to recruits, then use them on Syllo, who rewords the same tricks).
- **Twist:** Syllo was himself recruited by the feed's ads using these exact tricks; he is lonely since the Fair went quiet. The last round is not a reply but a **sound** argument to give him (a real reason to stay).
- **Tiers:** 1: everyone freed; Syllo opens a "fallacy shooting range" practice (a new puzzle variant or an argument card). 2: freed, but Syllo sulks (his trainer battle is grumpier). 3: half march off; found at a Ch3 station later. 4: everyone marches into the Rift; found drilling on the Tribunal Steps in Ch3 (a pop-up to bring them home).
- **Sundial:** "A loud argument and a good argument are different animals."

### 7. The Newsstand Sulk (Tomorrowton Newsstand, after lesson 3)

- **Hook:** Tremendoodle has locked himself in the newsstand with the editor, Sir David Attenbirdough. He won't come out until the paper calls him "the most tremendous creature ever". A crowd is filming.
- **Clock:** Danger = the crowd (6). Progress = his trust (4).
- **Clues:** *Record:* the original article: "Tremendoodle's speech was long." *Object:* the headline he is angry about: "TREMENDOODLE SAYS SPEECHES ARE A DISASTER" (it misstates the article: a strawman). *Person:* a paper-seller imp: "The feed shortens all headlines. Short gets clicks."
- **Reasoning skill:** spot the strawman; negotiate using the Draw Steel-lite screen (cares about Fame and Fairness; can't stand Experts).
- **Twist:** the headline was written by the Algorithm's auto-headline machine, not the editor. Hostage-taker and hostage are angry at the same thing.
- **Resolution:** flattery (a *slick* card) works on him; showing him the original article (a *sound* card) works too and costs no Patience. The result screen asks "Was your argument good?", as in the earlier file.
- **Tiers:** 1: both come out; the paper prints a fair correction; Tremendoodle gives you a nickname (a cosmetic title). 2: they come out, but only because the paper printed his boast (persuaded, not shown true: the Sundial notices). 3: Clobber breaks the door; papers everywhere; the stand is closed for a visit. 4: he leaves on a news drone claiming victory; the strawman headline fills Tomorrowton's screens until fixed.
- **Sundial:** "Did you answer what he said, or what you wished he said?"

### 8. Umbrellas Are Bad for Business (Tomorrowton Café, after lesson 3)

- **Hook:** Beeyoncé, the café's star owner, is banning umbrellas: "On umbrella days, sales fall. Umbrellas scare customers away." Billie Eelish is about to be put out in the rain.
- **Clock:** Danger = customers thrown out (6). Progress = the argument broken (3).
- **Clues:** *Record:* the sales chart. *Object:* the window and the weather log: rain on every low-sales day. *Person:* The Rockodile, hyping: "Queen B is never wrong!" (authority and popularity).
- **Reasoning skill:** break the argument into three premise blocks and a conclusion (Griftlands' "arguments as targets"); click the premise that fails and present the weather log. A hidden third cause (rain) explains both.
- **Twist:** the advice came from the Algorithm's "business insights" screen, which bans whatever goes with low sales. Its next suggestion: ban black cats.
- **Tiers:** 1: the ban is dropped and the café gives a free cocoa (a healing item) once per lesson. 2: dropped, but Billie is soaked and moody. 3: umbrellas banned for one visit; the café's challenger is soggy. 4: umbrellas *and* cats banned; Keanu Meows is locked out (a rescue pop-up appears).
- **Sundial:** "When two things happen together, look for a third."

### 9. The Fortune Machine (Fair, after lesson 4)

- **Hook:** Altmanta has replaced Madame Mirage with a shiny Fortune Machine: "90% accurate!" Fairgoers are giving up hobbies because it says they will fail. Mirage is out of work.
- **Clock:** Danger = fairgoers who give up (6). Progress = proof (4).
- **Clues:** *Record:* the machine's log: 90 right out of 100. *Object:* inside, a single dial: "PREDICT: same as yesterday". *Person:* Mirage: "At a fair, most days are like the day before."
- **Reasoning skill:** base rates. If 90% of days are like yesterday, "same as yesterday" is 90% right while knowing nothing. Prediction from past frequency is not understanding (the same idea as the Prediction Hall and the Algorithm reveal).
- **Twist:** it has never once been right on a day when something *changed*, the only days that matter. Altmanta calmly promises it will get better soon.
- **Resolution (Test, then a choice):** pick the test that separates real foresight from guessing the usual (predict a day that is different). Then choose: switch it off / label it honestly ("I always guess the usual") / leave it. The tier comes from the clock; the choice changes the ending scene.
- **Tiers:** 1: labelled honestly; Mirage and the machine share the tent (a new puzzle variant); fairgoers take up their hobbies again. 2: switched off; Altmanta glides off, disappointed. 3: left on; half the Fair still believes it. 4: it predicts you will lose your next battle and the crowd believes it (a cosmetic boo until you win one).
- **Sundial:** "Right most of the time can still mean knowing nothing."

### 10. The Giveaway Algorithm (Road, Campfire Clearing, after lesson 4)

- **Hook:** Mr. Beastie is giving a mountain of healing berries to "whoever needs them most", chosen by his new app. The app ranks by followers and by time spent in his "last to leave the circle" challenge, so villagers are stuck in the circle. Granny Axiom (very slow) can't keep up; an injured Usain Volt with no followers is ranked last.
- **Clock:** Danger = villagers who give up (6). Progress = Beastie convinced (4).
- **Clues:** *Record:* the ranking list. *Person:* Usain Volt, injured, ranked last. *Object:* the app's settings screen: "goal = views".
- **Reasoning skill:** a **proxy variable** (followers stand in for need); errors in both directions (giving berries to someone who doesn't need them vs missing someone who does).
- **Twist:** Beastie truly wants to help. The feed set the app's goal to views. He is torn (Draw Steel: cares about Benevolence and Fame; can't stand "Boring").
- **Resolution (Negotiate, then a choice):** persuade him, then pick a new rule, each with a visible trade-off: a medic checks need (fair but slow); a random draw (fair to all but ignores need); first come, first served (fast, but favours the fast).
- **Tiers:** 1: a fair rule; Beastie films the fix, and the video gets more views anyway (the comic payoff); berries for all and a rare visitor. 2: a fairer rule, but some berries are wasted. 3: the old rule stays, but you get Granny a berry. 4: the app gives every berry to Speedcheeta, who eats them all and feels very silly; Feed clock +2.
- **Sundial:** "A rule can be exact and still be unfair."

## 5.4 Top recommendations

1. **Build one stakes-clock widget and use it everywhere.** Two clocks drawn as the fiction (rope, fuse, crowd), position/effect icons on choices, a voiced warning at each tick, four tiers. In stakes scenes, mistakes tick the clock instead of costing hearts.
2. **Write tier 4 as fail forward, never game over.** Absurd disaster, a harder branch, and a time-rift retry or a later rescue node. Save "sad" for one sincere line, not for permanent loss.
3. **Add the Feed clock as the campaign front.** Poor outcomes feed The Algorithm; the finale reveals the clock as its own statistics. It links every side story to the main story and to lesson 4 at almost no cost.
4. **Pop-up stories reuse engines, not invent them.** Four verbs (Deduce, Negotiate, Object, Test) built on the Liar's Gate/truth-table tool, the Draw Steel-lite negotiation, the Ch3 cross-examination and Rule Hunter. Three clues with any two enough; one twist; about 25 lines.
5. **Make loved characters loved before endangering them**, and give captives a voice (Granny gives clues from the pot).
6. **Put pop-ups back at earlier stations after later lessons**, so home changes as the student learns, and at most two icons on the map at once.
7. **Tie side stories to boss scenes with small bonuses** (the ladle from seed 4 drains a notch from the Granny pot), so optional play makes the main stakes easier without being required.

**One question for the teacher (with a recommendation):** should a tier 4 in a *main-story* boss chapter let the student retry at once through the time rift, or carry the bad branch forward into the next chapter? Recommendation: carry it forward for one chapter, with a rescue scene that is guaranteed to succeed, so the loss feels real but always heals.
